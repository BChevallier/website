Five questions.

  • What makes a fish a fish?
  • What makes a continent a continent?
  • What makes a country a country?
  • What makes a vegetable a vegetable?
  • What makes a sport a sport?
Five everyday words that everybody uses and nobody can define cleanly. The following slides try to find a definition for each of them.

What makes a fish a fish?

  • Historically, animal groups (mammals, reptiles, birds, etc.) were defined by convention and common traits.
  • Biologists later started classifying animals by common ancestry.
  • Animals we call "fish" are very far apart evolutionarily.
  • A common ancestor would also include all mammals. That's a problem.
The common ancestor of salmon and sharks lived roughly 450 million years ago. Salmon are more closely related to humans than to sharks. A group defined by common ancestry has to contain all descendants of that ancestor, which would put mammals (that includes us) inside "fish".

Let's ask Wikipedia.

A fish is an aquatic, anamniotic, gill-bearing vertebrate animal with a tough cranium to protect the brain, but lacking limbs with digits.
Paraphrased from the English Wikipedia article on fish. Each highlighted phrase is one criterion of the definition.

Included by the definition

Hagfish, seahorses, flying fish and mudskippers look nothing like a typical fish, but all fit the definition. Clicking an image opens its source. Excluded by the definition

Whales (mammals), cuttlefish (molluscs), jellyfish (cnidarians) and starfish (echinoderms) are not vertebrates with gills and a cranium, so the definition excludes them even though "fish" is in the name of two of them. What makes a continent a continent?

Next question: continents.

Let's ask Wikipedia.

[...] Continents are generally identified by convention rather than any strict criteria. [...]

Well, thanks for nothing...

Quote from the English Wikipedia article on continents.

Let's use a classroom definition.

Continents are large landmasses separated from others by oceans.
Map: "Continents by colour", CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; clicking it opens the source. Under this definition Europe and Asia would be one continent (Eurasia), and North and South America are joined by a land bridge, as are Asia and Africa.

Let's ask geologists.

Different countries and fields count different numbers of continents (anywhere from four to seven). Geologists, for example, look at tectonic plates, which gives yet another answer. Map: USGS (public domain), via Wikimedia Commons; clicking it opens the source.

What makes a country a country?

  • 193 UN member states
  • 7 additional partially recognized countries
  • 206 participating countries at the Olympics
  • Our definition needs to include the Vatican and exclude Hong Kong.
Different lists, different numbers. Which one counts depends on who is asking and why. The Vatican is a UN observer state; Hong Kong competes at the Olympics as its own team.

Let's look at common characteristics.

  • defined territory (or not)
  • permanent population (or not)
  • self-governing (or not)
  • international recognition (or not)
  • own currency (or not)
  • independent military (or not)
Typical criteria for statehood. None of them is both necessary and sufficient.

Some edge cases

The Vatican has almost no territory or permanent population. Hong Kong has its own currency, laws and Olympic team but is part of China. Greenland governs itself but belongs to the Kingdom of Denmark. Somaliland governs itself and has no international recognition. The images are the flags of the Vatican, Hong Kong, Greenland and Somaliland; clicking one opens its Wikimedia Commons page.

What makes a vegetable a vegetable?

Next question: vegetables.

Let's ask Wikipedia.

Vegetables are edible parts of plants that are consumed by humans or other animals as food.

How wonderfully vague...

Paraphrased from the English Wikipedia article on vegetables.

Why is it so hard to define?

  • Because it's not a botanical term, but a culinary one.
  • In botany we differentiate between seeds, roots, stems, flowers, fruits... and that's it. No vegetables.
  • So saying "a tomato is a fruit, not a vegetable" is a category error.
Botanically, a tomato is a fruit, but "vegetable" is a kitchen category, so the two statements do not contradict each other.

Is there a culinary definition?

  • In short: no.
  • Culinary classifications are not standardized and vary enormously between individuals and cultures.
  • The best "definition" I could find is: a plant we eat, but not for dessert.
The "not for dessert" rule is a rule of thumb, not a standard. It is also what makes rhubarb awkward.

Some edge cases

Mushrooms are fungi, rhubarb is a stalk we eat in desserts, avocado is a fruit we eat in savoury dishes, and nori is algae. Clicking an image opens its source. What makes a sport a sport?

Last question: sports.

Let's ask Wikipedia.

Sport is a physical activity or game, often competitive and organized, that maintains or improves physical ability and skills.

That's not very helpful.

Paraphrased from the English Wikipedia article on sport. The highlighted words ("or", "often") show how many exits the definition leaves open.

Let's look at common characteristics.

  • form of physical activity (or not)
  • competitive (or not)
  • done for its own sake (or not)
  • having clear rules (or not)
  • social (or not)
As with countries, none of these is both necessary and sufficient.

Some edge cases

Chess, darts, fishing and esports are each called a sport by some people and not by others. Clicking an image opens its source. Chess: photo by Wilfredor, CC0. Darts: photo by Kurt Kaiser, CC0. Five questions. One answer.

  • What makes a fish a fish? We do.
  • What makes a continent a continent? We do.
  • What makes a country a country? We do.
  • What makes a vegetable a vegetable? We do.
  • What makes a sport a sport? We do.
These categories are statements about human convention, not about nature. Nature contains animals, landmasses, plants and activities; we are the ones who draw the lines between them.

Categories compress information.

  • "Fish" saves you from listing every trait of every animal in the water.
  • "Vegetable" tells a cook roughly what to expect, in one word.
  • A category is a shared shortcut: we agree on it so we don't have to explain.
A category bundles many properties into one word. If speaker and listener share the same convention, one word transmits what would otherwise take a whole description. That is the entire job of a category.

Compression is lossy.

  • A category hides differences between its members.
  • Hagfish and salmon are both "fish", yet they share less than salmon and humans do.
  • The edge cases are where the information gets lost.
Like a compressed image, a category throws away detail. Usually that is fine. At the edges (the Vatican, rhubarb, chess) the lost detail is exactly what we are arguing about, which is why the definitions never settle.

When it stops compressing, discard it.

  • At the fishmonger, "fish" still works.
  • In evolutionary biology, it doesn't. Use clades instead.
  • Same word, different job. Keep it where it helps, drop it where it misleads.
A category is judged by whether it still saves communication effort in a given context. When describing what to buy for dinner, "fish" does. When describing ancestry, it causes confusion, and a more specific term (a clade such as ray-finned fishes, or vertebrates) is better.

Words are tools...

  • Tools should make life easier.
  • If they don't, they shouldn't be used.
  • Specific tools are made for specific situations.
A word is useful if it makes communication easier. If it causes more confusion than it removes, drop it or replace it with a more specific word.
